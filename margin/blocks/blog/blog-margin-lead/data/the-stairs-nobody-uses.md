There is a stairway at the end of my street that I walked past for three years before I climbed it. It begins between two houses, behind a gate that is never locked, and it ends ninety-one steps later on a street I had always reached the long way round, by car, in four minutes.

<aside>I counted twice, once going up and once coming down, and got ninety-one and ninety-two. I'm going with the lower number, the one I was less tired for.</aside>

Public stairs are the city's footnotes. They were built when people walked to work, to the mills and the streetcar lines at the bottom of the hill, and they were built as shortcuts: a straight line where the streets had to wind. Most of them are still public rights of way. Almost nobody uses them.

## A month of climbing

In October I made a list of every stairway I could find, from old maps, from the city's own records and from asking at the hardware store, and I set out to climb one each morning before work. I found twenty-six. Six are closed, two are someone's garden now, and one turned out to be a fire escape.

> A stairway is a promise someone once made: that people would come this way on foot, often enough to be worth the stone.

What I didn't expect was how much each one says about the hill it climbs. The old ones are granite, worn into a dip in the middle where a hundred years of shoes went; the newer ones are poured concrete with a steel rail, built after a slope gave way or a road was cut through. A few have <mark>a landing halfway up with a bench</mark>, which tells you that whoever built them expected the old and the tired, and wanted them to stop and look.

![Downtown Providence across the river, seen from the top of a stairway](/media/footbridge.jpg)

From the top of the longest one you see the whole of downtown across the river, the footbridge low over the water, the State House behind. It is the best view in the city that you cannot drive to.

## Who climbs them

I kept count, as I do. In twenty-six mornings I met forty-one people on the stairs: dog walkers mostly, a nurse coming off a night shift, two boys carrying an amplifier between them, and a man in a suit who told me he had climbed the same flight every day for eleven years and had never once seen anyone else on it.

<aside>He asked me not to say which flight. It's his, he said, the way a bench in a park can be yours.</aside>

That's the thing about the stairs nobody uses. Somebody always does. They are kept alive by the few people who know them, and they disappear, quietly, when those few move away and nobody sweeps the leaves.

If you know a stairway I missed, write and tell me where. I'll climb it before the snow.
