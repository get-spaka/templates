Every city has a river it would rather not talk about. Ours is not one river but several, and for most of the last century the place where they met downtown was a deck of roads and parking, the water running dark beneath it.

They were uncovered at the end of the century, and now there are bridges and a park and, on summer nights, fires on the water. But the rivers went under in more places than downtown, and some of them have never come back up.

<aside>The smaller streams are the ones that stayed buried. A river has a name and friends; a brook has a culvert.</aside>

## Following the low ground

A buried stream is easy to find once you know how to look. The street dips where it runs. The houses on either side have damp cellars. There is a grate in the road where you can hear water on a quiet morning, and somewhere downhill there is a concrete mouth where it comes out into the light.

![The river through Providence in 1900, lined with wharves](/media/river-1900.jpg)

I walked the low ground for three Sundays with an old map folded in my pocket, and on the third I found the mouth: a stone arch behind a parking lot, the water coming out of it clear and cold and surprisingly loud.

> You can pave over a river, but you can't stop it from running downhill.
